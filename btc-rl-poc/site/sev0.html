SEV-0 — where the error is born, live MITIGATING

A systematic bias (a built-in lean toward "price goes up") was traced to its origin and measured at every layer of the system. This page recomputes every number below from the raw ledgers on each refresh — the same data the daemon trades on. Full remediation spec with citations: docs/SEV0_REMEDIATION.md in the repo.

Executive summary — what happened, what was decided

The desk lost money on 08/26 while winning 65.6% of its bets, because break-even at the prices paid was 66.7%. The audit (tests/sev0_error_audit.py) walked the full architecture and found the loss was not one broken part but one small bias amplified by every layer above it. 95% of lost dollars carry a shared, taggable cause; only 5% were idiosyncratic.

2 · The bias is a moving target — which changes the M1 design

Splitting each arm's history in half and refitting the correction curve separately: the intercept and slope move between halves for most arms. Overnight the family-wide optimism halved (kb7 a −0.57 → −0.35) while slopes fell below 1 — the error mode shifted from "systematically optimistic" to "overconfident spread". Consequence: a one-time correction constant would already be stale. M1 must be an online tracker, refit every settle — which is how it was specced (Platt 1999 fit prequentially, Dawid 1984), now with direct evidence rather than assumption.

3 · The best fixed arm is not stable — which vindicates M3

Yesterday's counterfactual said "just hold kb4" (+4.7%/$1, best of eight). On the fuller sample kb4 is negative and kb9 is the only arm above water. Anyone who had hard-coded yesterday's winner would be losing money today. Consequence: this is the exact setting Fixed-Share was designed for — tracking the best sequence of experts, not the best fixed one (Herbster & Warmuth 1998). M3's priority rises; "pick the best arm and freeze it" is now explicitly rejected.

5 · Two claims tested before changing anything — one failed

Was 08/27 detectable in advance? Yes. Trailing-20-window market accuracy (an ex-ante signal — no hindsight) below 0.62 flagged 63.6% of 08/27's windows before entry, versus 5.1% on the good day 08/28. Market Brier confirms it: 0.224 on 08/27 vs 0.167 on 08/28. That discrimination earns a new mitigation (M8, regime gate) — though 08/26 also flagged 59% while finishing profitable, so it needs a proper backtest before it trades.

Is the "toxic hour" real? No — and M7 is dropped. Hours 09h and 14h looked persistently bad (bad on 2 of 3 days). But we searched 24 hours: at the desk's 32.5% loss rate, a 3–4 bid day-cell looks "bad" 17.6% of the time, so an hour looks bad on ≥2 of 3 days 8.2% of the time by luck — expected count from chance alone 1.98 of 24. We observed 2. P(≥2 | chance) = 0.60. Shipping M7 would have been data dredging. It is removed from the plan, not deferred.

6 · Correction to our own root-cause chain supersedes 08/26

Asked whether the RL tier is tracked as well as the rest, we found it is (every arm scored 99.2–100%, zero staleness) — and in checking, found that most RL arms never reach a trading decision. t2, t6, t7, t8, t9, t10, t11 are logged-only; the binary tiers run off the consensus/horizon path plus the market anchor. The consequence is uncomfortable and worth stating plainly: the bullish drift we named as the root cause on 08/26 (t10 +$29, t11 +$41 bias @h15) lives entirely in arms that never place a bet. The path that does feed decisions is near-unbiased (+$1.76 mean bias). Tier-1 directional drift cannot be the source of tier-2 optimism.

What survives, and is now the corrected chain: the decision-feeding arms' 80% bands only cover 74–76% — σ is too small. Converting a too-narrow forecast distribution into P(above strike) pushes probabilities toward the extremes, which is exactly the slope b < 1 measured at tier 2. It is a dispersion defect, not a direction defect. M4 is re-aimed accordingly: the drift-recentering half is dropped (cosmetic — those arms trade nothing) and the adaptive-conformal band fix is promoted to the real tier-1 work.

7 · Is any arm pure poison as a leader?

No arm has zero wins — but three lose money when leading: kb8 (34 bids, 58.8% win vs 67.1% break-even, −$494), kb2 (−$333) and the already-retired kb6 (−$364). None reach significance (p = 0.20, 0.13, 0.46), so none can be banned on this evidence — flagged for watch, not action. kb9 (+$275) and kb4 (+$195) are the profitable leaders.

The decision-level scan turned up something more valuable: kb5 wins only 44.7% of its calls but returns +16.7% per $1 staked — the best EV of any arm, because it buys deep longshots where break-even is far below 50%. It is simultaneously our most-wrong caller and our most profitable one. That reopens the cheap-bid "Underdog" trader we scoped but never launched (M9).

Champion vs challengers — live on real traffic …

Every improvement now runs as a treatment on real live windows, paired against the incumbent desk policy, and is promoted only when the live stream clears a sequential test — never on backtest evidence. Three of our own confident offline conclusions have already been wrong (the tier-1 root cause, the "toxic hour", the calibration premise), so nothing is adopted on a replay again.

How it is judged. All policies see the same window and the same settled outcome, so the market regime cancels out of the difference — 08/27 proved an unpaired comparison can be swamped by one bad day. Effective n is windows. The test is Wald's SPRT (1945): H₀ "no better" vs H₁ "better by ≥2%/$1", accumulating evidence until it crosses a boundary — valid under continuous monitoring, unlike a fixed-horizon test re-checked every window. Standing down is a real decision that scores 0 and risks nothing, which is how a veto treatment can win without placing a single bet.

A 2.1¢ bug was manufacturing almost every "win" here. The champion was first scored at the real ask it paid while challengers were priced from the modelled quote — a systematic 2.1¢ discount to the challengers. Under that bug M3 read +23.5% against the champion. Pricing every policy from the same decision-time quote collapsed it to +1.2%; M9 went +10.6% → −5.0%, M2 +8.0% → −8.6%, M1 −3.7% → −26.1%. Only M8 and M3 remain positive at all, both far from significance. Had the framework shipped one commit earlier it would have auto-promoted a pricing artifact into live trading.

Read the absolute EV column with care: it is now model-priced from the quote midpoint plus the standard spread, so it runs optimistic against the fills the desk actually gets. The paired difference is the number the SPRT tests and the only one that is apples-to-apples.

M1 shadow — is calibration actually earning its place? shadow

The calibration layer is live but trading nothing: every kb row now carries p_cal beside p_up, and each arm's (a, b) refits on a 150-window sliding window. The verdict below is prequential — each window is scored by the fit that existed before it settled, then trained on (Dawid 1984). LL is decayed mean log-loss; lower is better, so gain = raw − calibrated must be positive for the layer to justify itself.

Honest status: on the warm-start replay calibration helped only kb7 — the most miscalibrated arm — and was marginally worse on the other eight. The premise is not yet confirmed. This is precisely why M1 runs in shadow rather than shipping: had we wired it straight into the decision tier on the strength of the 08/26 audit, we would have shipped a regression. The gate stands: it earns the switch-over or it is dropped.
